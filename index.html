Build your requirements diagrams one decision at a time

A method guide for Part 1.2 of the patient monitoring coursework. It covers the notation, the seven relationships, the difference between high-level and low-level requirements, and how the choices you make now carry into your BDDs and the 2.5 conformance mapping. It teaches how. Your requirements, names and links stay yours to write.

Every complete diagram here uses a fictional greenhouse climate controller, so nothing on this page is coursework content. Notation follows SysML 1.6 and 1.7 and is tool-neutral.

The chevrons show the order you work in. SysML dependency arrows point the other way: from the newer element back to the one it depends on. A low-level requirement points at its high-level parent, and a block points at the requirement it satisfies.

What a requirement box and a diagram frame must show

Correct SysML notation is one of the five things Part 1.2 is marked on. These are the parts a marker will look for.

  1. 1
    «requirement» keyword. It tells the reader this box is a requirement element. In SysML 1.x a requirement is a stereotyped class.
  2. 2
    Name. A short label for the capability, shown in bold. It is a noun phrase, not a sentence.
  3. 3
    id property. Your identifier: unique, never reused, and the same in your spreadsheet, every diagram and your report text.
  4. 4
    text property. The requirement statement itself, with one “shall”.

The frame header

SysML requires every diagram to sit in a frame. Its header tag reads diagramKind [modelElementType] modelElementName [diagramName]:

req[Package]GCC Requirements[High-Level Requirements]
req
The diagram kind. Requirement diagrams are req, block definition diagrams bdd, sequence diagrams sd.
[Package]
The type of model element that owns the diagram, usually the package your requirements live in.
GCC Requirements
That element’s name.
[High-Level Requirements]
The diagram’s own name, which you choose. It is how the reader tells your two requirement diagrams apart.

One element, many views

A requirement exists once in the model. Each diagram is a view of it. To show a high-level requirement on the low-level diagram, drag the existing element in from the model browser. Never draw a new box with the same name: a duplicate breaks every traceability table built from the model.

Properties, not typed labels

Tools display id and text differently. Some show Id = "…" in a compartment, some put the text in the box body. What matters is that id and text are set as the element’s properties, not typed into the name field.

Seven relationships, and which end the arrowhead goes on

The direction rule. Every dependency (deriveReqt, refine, satisfy, verify, trace, copy) is a dashed line with an open arrowhead. It starts at the element that depends and ends on the requirement it depends on. Containment is different: a solid line with a crosshair at the parent.

Text compartments are left out of these small diagrams to save space.

Choosing the link: answer one question at a time

Use this whenever you are about to draw a line and are not sure which kind it is.

“Refine” in the brief is plain English

The brief asks you to “refine the model” with low-level requirements. That means make it more detailed. It is not an instruction to use the «refine» keyword. In SysML terms, a new and more specific requirement that you worked out from a high-level one and an Article provision is usually a «deriveReqt». The specification’s own example is lower-level requirements derived from a system requirement.

Whichever relationship you choose, state the convention once (in a comment on the diagram or a line in your report) and apply it to every link.

Containment or derive?

Containment says the children together are the parent, split into single pieces. Derive says the new requirement adds detail the parent never stated. Your low-level requirements cover only part of each high-level requirement, and they add conformance detail from the Act, so derive usually fits better. Containment would claim your 4–6 requirements fully cover their parents.

Three ways to show which Article provision a low-level requirement came from

A. «rationale» comment

Anchor a «rationale» comment to each «deriveReqt» line, citing the paragraph (for example a provision of Article 14(4)). SysML lets a rationale attach to relationships as well as elements.

For: keeps your requirement count clean at 4–6. Against: the provision is text, not a model element, so a traceability table will not list it.

B. Provisions as source elements

Put each cited provision in its own package as a source requirement, and draw «deriveReqt» from your low-level requirement to it as well as to the high-level parent.

For: the first link of the 2.5 chain becomes a real model link. Against: adds boxes. Label them clearly as sources (separate package, own ID prefix) so they are not counted as your 4–6.

C. In the ID or text only

Encode the article in the ID or mention it inside the requirement text.

For: quick. Against: weakest: nothing in the model connects the requirement to its source. Use it alongside A or B, not instead of them.

High-level says what the system must achieve; low-level says what it must do so a provision can be met

Both levels describe the problem. Neither chooses technology. The marking criteria call this “separation between the problem and implementation decisions”.

Too high, about right, too low

Greenhouse example, about water.

Too vague
The GCC shall manage water sensibly.
Nothing to test: “sensibly” has no observable meaning.
High-level
The GCC shall adjust the climate of any zone that is outside its set ranges.
A principal need, open to any technology.
Low-level
The GCC shall limit the water delivered to each zone to that zone’s daily water allowance.
Narrower and testable, still no mechanism.
Too low
The GCC shall close a solenoid valve over MQTT when the flow meter passes 40 litres.
A design decision. The valve and the protocol belong in a BDD block.

Quick test: if a different technology could still meet the statement, it is a requirement. If only one design could, it is a design decision.

Whichever you pick: IDs are unique, never reused, and never renumbered once your report cites them. The worked example below uses a source-coded variant (W for its water clause, M for misting).

Name

  • A noun phrase of two to five words in Title Case, naming the capability
  • No “shall”: the name is a label, the text is the requirement
  • Distinct from its siblings, so the diagram reads on its own

Text

  • One “shall” per requirement. Two means two requirements.
  • Active voice, with your system as the subject
  • Use the same system name in every requirement, your BDD system block and your sequence lifeline; 2.5 marks consistency across models
  • Verifiable: someone could check it was met
  • No technology, products, algorithms or screen widgets

Verifiable without clinical numbers

The case study says you are not assessed on clinical thresholds. You can keep a requirement testable by referring to a value the deploying organisation sets, instead of inventing a number.

    Your Patient Data Risk Map already holds three kinds of material, and each has a different job in SysML:

    Article paragraphs = sources

    The lit paragraphs of Article 10 and Article 14(4) are where low-level requirements come from. Cite them using your chosen convention (A or B above).

    Risks (R10-, R14-) = rationale

    A risk explains why a requirement is needed. It belongs in your 1.1 prose and in rationale comments, not in requirement text.

    Measures (M1–M11) = design responses

    A measure is an answer in solution terms: a register, a gate, a panel. It becomes a block in the low-level BDD that «satisfy»s a requirement. It is not itself a requirement.
